    Cheats and the Game Master Console

    Seventeen tools (nineteen on beta) for rewriting the world by hand — the GM console, standing reminders, events, territory, countries and forces.

    The Cheats panel is the game master's toolbox: seventeen tools for reaching into the world and changing it by hand — including direct control of forces, which lives here rather than in the normal interface.

    Calling it "cheats" undersells it. Most of these are how you keep a long campaign on the rails when the simulation drifts, or how you author a world deliberately.

    Open it from the game menu: ☰ → Tools → Cheats.

    On beta there are nineteen. Groups and Puppet States join the Countries & Territory tools; both are described below.

    Cheats, the events timeline and the AI debug console, one click from the game menu.

    The tools, grouped: GM & History, Countries & Territory, Military, Simulation and Map. The GM Console is the one to reach for first.

    GM Console

    The most useful tool on the list. Describe a change in plain English and the AI plans it as a structured transaction — territory, countries, units, structures, wars, relations, agreements, storylines — and every operation is previewed before anything happens. Apply commits exactly that preview, with an audit record, and rolls back if the write fails. A change that would not land is refused with the reason, rather than quietly doing nothing.

    "Hungary leaves the alliance and signs a non-aggression pact with Serbia. Their relationship with Vienna sours."

    Pick a mode first:

    Mode For
    Direct / OOC correction Exact edits with the smallest possible scope, such as setting a country's population, with no timeline event
    Exact Event One timeline event, written as you describe it, with everything it establishes
    World Intervention A coherent change of several events across territory, countries, wars, diplomacy, forces and statistics

    Apply makes no second AI request, checks the live world again just before it writes, and never advances the date or the round.

    It works because it goes through the same machinery events do, so whatever it does is a legitimate world state rather than a hand-edited file.

    Use it when something has gone wrong that no single tool fixes, or when you want to steer the narrative rather than wait for the simulation to get there.

    On beta the preview lists every kind of change a transaction holds, governments and parties, groups, institutions and puppet states among them, so nothing it would do is left out of what you approve.

    The next skip is told

    Every change made with these tools — a country annexed, a border redrawn, figures set by hand, an event written, the history document rewritten, a turn rolled back — is recorded as one line, and the next time skip opens with them: changes made outside the simulation since your last turn. They are canon, acts of authority rather than events, not to be undone or written up again. The advisor's catch-up note carries them too. None of it costs a request.

    Simulation Reminders

    Standing facts every AI in the game is told until you withdraw them — "the Kerch bridge is down". A short, dated list; every prompt that writes the world or speaks for a country ends with it, the advisor and every leader included. Edit or withdraw one here; withdrawing one is itself a change, so the next skip is told the fact no longer holds. Every AI sees every reminder, so a secret does not belong here.

    Turn and campaign control

    Roll Back Turn restores the world to the start of any earlier turn, discarding everything after it. Broader than the Undo in the time panel, which only steps back one turn at a time.

    Play As Country switches which country you play. You can hand yourself a different nation mid-campaign, or follow a war from the other side.

    Territory

    Annex Country — click a country on the map and all of its regions fold into a target.

    Annex Regions — click regions one at a time to transfer them individually.

    Both enter a click-capture mode: map clicks go to the tool instead of opening the usual popups, and the panel gets out of the way until you are done.

    Region Inspector shows any region's controller, sovereign and claimants, and how each came to be — and lets you edit control and make legal transfers. See territory.

    Countries

    Country Editor opens a country: its name and colour and, once it has a stat sheet, the sheet itself — population, GDP, capital, currency, stability, inflation, unemployment, debt, budget balance, the strategic indices with international reputation among them, and the GDP sector split. Pick the country on the map, or from the list. Useful when the simulation has drifted a country somewhere you do not want it, or when you want to write a country's character deliberately. Tags are not edited here: the GM console changes them, and a scenario's starting tags are set in the map editor.

    On beta the Country Editor also holds the country's Political World: its government and political system, its strategic outlook (goals, fears, ambitions, pressures), its parties and power blocs, and its political traits.

    Add Country creates a new one from nothing: a name and a colour. It holds no land until you give it some with Annex Country or Annex Regions, and a name already in use is refused — changing an existing country is the Country Editor's job.

    Both work on any country, not just yours. See countries and identity.

    Groups (beta) lists the game's groups and how many regions each controls. New group takes a name, what it is — which the AI is told — and a tint colour; Edit the area on the map then puts each region you click under the group, or takes it out again. Clear the whole area and Erase group (click twice) undo it. The tool is hidden while groups are switched off for the game.

    Puppet States (beta) is the ledger of who answers to whom, by hand and at no AI request: each puppet with its overlord, its kind (protectorate, puppet state or client state), whether it is covert or openly known, and its loyalty. New puppet state picks the overlord, the puppet, the kind, whether it is known and the loyalty; set loyalty below 35 and the panel warns that the puppet starts plotting against its overlord. A live arrangement can be edited, made public if it is covert, released, or annexed, which ends it and hands all of the puppet's land to its overlord; releasing and annexing each want a second click. Ended arrangements are kept in a list below.

    Map features

    Map Feature Editor and Add Map Feature work on cities and structures — position, name, population, kind, owner, status. Place cities, HQs, landmarks, ports and other features. The editor keeps two lists, World features and Scenario cities.

    Clear Map Features removes accumulated markers that have stopped mattering — long campaigns build up a lot of them — and can delete a scenario's own cities, or switch the scenario back to the standard world cities.

    In a scenario with its own city list, a city you add joins that list with its population and tier. The stock world cities cannot be changed in play, so in a scenario that uses them a new city is added as a world feature instead, without a population or tier.

    On beta, setting the population of a city whose scenario gives it by year makes your figure the city's population from then on. See cities and structures.

    Events and history

    The Event Editor searches, creates, edits and deletes canonical events. An event you write can quote people, carry metadata, and optionally draw an NPC reaction after a 12-second undo window.

    This is the tool for repairing history. If a turn produced an event that contradicts everything before it, you can rewrite it rather than rolling back and re-rolling the whole turn. You can also author events outright, which is how you run a scripted campaign.

    Because events carry the world changes, editing one is editing history in both senses.

    Interactive Event plays any event on the record out as a scene, the way a time skip offers one now and then. See interactive events.

    History Document is the living account of the campaign the AI is shown in place of older events. Read it, and edit it — anything you write there is what the next skip believes about the past. The timeline itself keeps every event. See what the world remembers.

    Forces

    Force Manager opens the Forces panel: deploy, inspect, edit and repair forces on the map — type, strength and composition. See military and combat.

    It is filed here deliberately. In normal play you order a military outcome and the world carries it out; placing divisions by hand is a game-master act.

    On beta, troops fighting on a battlefield are commanded in normal play too, from the War Room, whose button appears while your troops are on one. The Force Manager is still where the map's units are placed and edited by hand.

    Difficulty

    Difficulty changes the level after the fact. See starting a game for what each level does.

    On beta, difficulty decides how things tend to go for you. On the easy levels your orders usually succeed and luck breaks your way; on Medium the world is realistic; on the hard levels your orders fall short and luck breaks against you. On battlefields it is also a stated handicap: above Medium the armies fighting you fight harder, advance faster and are reinforced more, and below it they are the ones held back. The change applies from the next AI decision.

    Should you use any of this?

    Yes. A game of your own has no score and nobody to cheat against. The tools exist because an AI-driven world sometimes produces something incoherent, and the alternative to fixing it is abandoning the campaign.

    The one habit worth keeping: prefer the GM console over the direct editors where both would work. Describing what happened produces a world that hangs together, including the knock-on consequences. Hand-editing a border produces a border that moved for no reason anyone in the world can remember.
